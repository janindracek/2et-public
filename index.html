Why the Czech State Fails to Support Its Homegrown Defence Industry

An issue tree of root causes, structured by the levers of state action

Root question

Why does the Czech state fail to convert its domestic defence industry into a competitive, scaling sector?

“Support” decomposes into four state functions: the state buys from the industry · helps it innovate · helps it export · and does not block it.

Scope & status: the tree isolates the state’s contribution — industry-side weaknesses (fragmentation, under-capitalisation, succession) are real but deliberately out of frame. And every box is a hypothesis, not a finding: the group’s job is to confirm or kill each one with first-hand evidence and data.

0 Strategy & political will
Upstream — sets the demand for everything below Won’t — a will problem
  • No clear, funded, multi-year sovereign-capability intent industry has no demand visibility to invest against
  • Capability needs framed around legacy platforms, not future threats what is absent from the strategy cannot be procured
  • Default “buy foreign off-the-shelf” reflex — interoperability plus ally signalling crowds out domestic suppliers
  • Short budget and political cycles no credible long-term commitment to plan against
  • No industrial-policy link between defence spend and domestic value-add or sovereignty the money leaves no industrial footprint

Bites hardest: platform & hardware primes — and starves every branch below.

1 Demand
The state as anchor customer Can’t — a capacity problem
  • Risk-averse, audit-driven culture: punished for failure, never rewarded for innovation defaults to incumbents and foreign primes
  • Lowest-price, spec-compliance tendering structurally excludes novel solutions
  • No fast or agile track — no SBIR equivalent, innovation-partnership procedure unused, no rapid pilots new entrants cannot get in at the speed they live or die at
  • No first-order anchor volume startups never win the reference contract they need to scale and raise against
  • Procurement cycle length technology is obsolete before award; startups die in the cash gap

Bites hardest: software & dual-use startups — speed and reference contracts.

2 Demand articulation
Does the buyer know what is possible? Can’t — a capacity problem
  • No demo days or experimentation venues the army cannot see what exists — so it cannot spec it, or desire it
  • Spec-led rather than problem-led procurement “here is our problem, propose solutions” is never asked
  • Weak feedback loops between end-user, procurement and industry field needs never reach the market
  • No structured tech-watch or horizon scanning inside the MoD the buyer does not know where the frontier is
  • Over-classification blocks the dialogue in both directions

Bites hardest: software & dual-use startups — novel categories the buyer has never seen.

3 Internal innovation capacity
Ability to absorb & test Can’t — a capacity problem
  • No defence innovation unit — no US DIU / UK DASA analogue with mandate, budget and risk tolerance
  • No accessible test ranges, certification or trial infrastructure nowhere to prove a product at home
  • No prototype → trial → adoption pathway the “valley of death” between demo and order
  • MoD lacks in-house engineers and technologists pay gap plus revolving door hollow out the smart-buyer function
  • Military conservatism and not-invented-here novelty is a career risk, not an asset

Bites hardest: software & dual-use startups — certification and test access.

4 Capital
De-risking & financing the technology Can’t — a capacity problem
  • Thin, fragmented national defence R&D budget no critical mass behind any priority
  • Weak absorption of EU instruments — EDF, SAFE, ASAP, EDIRPA limited capacity to win, co-finance and administer
  • No defence-tech VC or patient capital; ESG and banking exclusions private finance is choked at the source
  • Dual-use gap civilian drone, AI, cyber and materials innovation is not channelled into defence
  • No grant → procurement bridge winning R&D does not convert into orders

Bites both: startups (the VC gap) and primes (capital intensity).

5 Export enablement
Scaling beyond a small home market Won’t + can’t — mixed
  • A small home market makes export existential yet it is treated as the industry’s private problem
  • Slow, opaque export licensing; political squeamishness about end-users deals die in the queue
  • Weak G2G salesmanship, export credit and diplomatic backing outgunned by the French, Israeli and Korean state machines
  • Legacy concentration in Soviet-era ammunition and small arms absent from the future categories now being bought
  • No coordinated country brand or trade-fair presence Czech firms show up alone

Bites hardest: platform & hardware primes — though export is existential for everyone.

6 Institutional & regulatory environment
The rails everything runs on Blocks — active friction
  • IP rules let the state capture industry IP a disincentive to bring your best technology to the buyer
  • Security-clearance bottlenecks lock out SMEs, startups and foreign-owned firms
  • Certification and standards burden scaled for primes disproportionate for SMEs
  • State-owned enterprises (VOP CZ, LOM Praha) with an unclear role split distort the field for private firms
  • Fragmented ownership across MoD, MPO and MFA no single accountable owner of the problem

Bites everyone — friction is regressive: heaviest for SMEs and new entrants.

How to read this tree

Three lenses, applied in order. The first sorts the diagnosis, the second locates the pain, the third picks the battle.

1 Won’t / can’t / blocks

For each branch, ask whether the state fails because it does not want to act (0), cannot act (1–4), or actively obstructs (6). The fix follows the diagnosis — a capacity fix will not solve a will problem.

2 Overlay the industry segments

Software and dual-use startups bleed most from 1 (agility), 2 (demo days), 3 (certification & test) and 4 (VC). Platform and hardware primes bleed most from 0 (strategy), 4 (capital intensity) and 5 (export & offsets). This tells you where each fix actually bites.

3 Highest-leverage cluster

Hypothesis to test: 0 + 2 + 3. Strategy sets the demand, articulation reveals it, absorption converts it. Demand (1) and capital (4) are necessary, but largely downstream of those three.

From tree to choice — the working session

The tree is the input. The output of the session is a choice: one or two branches the group commits to work on.

1 Attack the tree

Each member confirms or kills causes from first-hand experience — where does your firm actually bleed? What is missing? A cause nobody in the room can evidence, and no data supports, is a candidate for deletion.

2 Locate today’s candidates

The group’s two current candidate measures already sit on the tree: faster certification → branches 3 + 6; EU-level procurement rules → branches 1 + 6. The question is no longer “are these good ideas?” but “are these the branches where the diagnosis says the leverage is?”

3 Pick the branch, then the measure

Choose where three things intersect: size of impact · members’ first-hand evidence · a credible 2ET role. Only then scope the specific measure on that branch — problem, benchmark, our role, natural carrier, resources.
